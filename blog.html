A physicist's map of German funding

Walter Benjamin, Emmy Noether, and the ERC Starting Grant, read side by side by Jeel Swami

Every research system has a handful of grants that quietly decide careers. In Germany, for anyone early in their path, three names come up again and again: the DFG's Walter Benjamin Programme, the DFG's Emmy Noether Programme, and the ERC Starting Grant. Ask around a physics institute and you will hear them treated like career stages rather than funding schemes. First you Benjamin, then you Noether, and if the stars align, you ERC.

What you will not easily find is a clear account of how the three actually differ, who can apply from where, and which clocks are ticking against you. The official information exists, and to the credit of the DFG and the ERC it is unusually thorough. But it lives in guideline PDFs with names like "form 50.10", in FAQ pages, and in work programme annexes that update every year. I went through all of it, the forms, the FAQs in both languages, and the questions international researchers keep asking in public forums, and built a small site that puts the rules in one place. This essay is the map that emerged.

Three programmes, three career moments

The cleanest way to understand the trio is not by budget but by the question each one answers.

Walter Benjamin asks: can you stand alone? It funds your own project for two years, either as a salaried position in Germany or as a fellowship abroad. You define the question, you pick the host, you answer for the result. The money is modest and the point is not the money. It is the first grant where the idea is yours.

Emmy Noether asks: can you lead? Six years, your own junior research group at a German institution, your own budget line with no upper limit, doctoral students who are yours to supervise. It exists as a deliberate alternative to the traditional German habilitation: pass through it and you are appointable to a professorship. The DFG even asks host universities to sign a model contract granting you the effective rights of a junior professor.

The ERC Starting Grant asks: can your idea change a field? Up to 1.5 million euros over five years, hosted anywhere in the EU or an associated country, judged on nothing but scientific ambition and your track record. Germany hosts more of these than any other country, 99 of the 478 awarded in the 2025 round.

The clocks are the trap

Here is the detail that catches people, including people with excellent advisors. Grant eligibility runs on "academic age", the years since your PhD, and the funders measure it with different clocks.

The ERC counts from the day you successfully defended. The DFG counts from your certificate date if the certificate arrived within a year of the defense, otherwise from the defense itself. If your university took eight months to print your certificate, your DFG window ends eight months later than your ERC window. For a borderline applicant that difference is a whole application cycle.

The windows themselves are strict. Emmy Noether wants your proposal within four years of the doctorate, six if you are a licensed physician or psychologist, with two extra years per child for women and one for men. Walter Benjamin has no numeric limit at all; it asks instead whether you are still in the "early postdoctoral phase", which ends the moment you have run your own projects or managed staff. And the ERC normally wants you between two and seven years after the defense.

Normally. This year is different, and it is worth saying plainly: for the 2027 call, with its deadline on 14 October 2026, the ERC widened the window to anywhere between zero and ten years past the defense. Researchers who thought they had permanently aged out get one more chance. Whether the window stays this wide after 2027 has not been published; what is certain is the career cap of one Starting Grant per lifetime that arrives in the same reform.

Five things I wish someone had told me earlier

First, the Walter Benjamin Programme runs in two directions, and only one of them is open to newcomers. The position variant, based in Germany, has no nationality requirement and no prior German connection at all; a postdoc in Mumbai or Boston can apply tomorrow. The fellowship variant, based abroad, is the opposite: it sends people out of the German system and requires you to already belong to it, through a mostly German education or three continuous years of research work in Germany, with fine print that can disqualify even German educated applicants after long stretches abroad. Forums are full of people discovering this the hard way.

Second, a stipend is not a salary. The fellowship variants of German funding, Walter Benjamin abroad and Humboldt among them, are usually tax free and feel generous, but they build no pension, carry no unemployment insurance, and leave health insurance entirely to you. German public pensions and salary scales also ignore stipend years later. An employment contract at the same net amount is nearly always worth more. Read this twice before choosing a variant.

Third, nobody checks your passport. All three programmes select on career stage and science, not citizenship. Emmy Noether was explicitly built to attract researchers from abroad into Germany; the ERC advertises worldwide and adds up to two million euros in extra budget if you relocate from outside Europe. The paperwork of moving, visas and insurance and pension accounts, is real, but it is paperwork, not eligibility.

Fourth, the decision takes longer than you think, so apply earlier than you planned. Walter Benjamin averages about six months from submission to decision, Emmy Noether about eight, and the ERC runs roughly ten months from deadline to final results, fourteen to signed grant. Apply in the last year of your window and you have bet everything on a single attempt. Apply early and a rejection is information rather than an ending.

Fifth, the host institution is a coauthor of your application, so treat it like one. The DFG wants employer statements and career support commitments; the ERC wants a binding letter, signed by the institution's legal representative, guaranteeing your scientific independence. Research offices need weeks for these, sometimes months, and they say no to strangers who appear five days before a deadline. The first email to a potential host is the real start of any application.

Where the map leads

None of this is secret. It is all in the official documents, every fact in this essay included, and I have linked each one to its source on the site rather than asking you to trust my summary. What was missing, as far as I could tell, was the connecting tissue: the rules read together, from the point of view of someone standing outside Germany and wondering whether the door is open.

It is. That is the honest summary of everything I read. Germany funds early career research at a scale and with an openness that surprises people, and the main thing standing between international researchers and these programmes is not eligibility but information.
